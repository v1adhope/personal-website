---
title: "Moderation needs clear communication"
pubDate: 2026-09-21
description: "Why pending and rejected submissions need explanations, next steps, expected wait times, and escalation paths."
tags: ["moderation", "userExperience"]
---

Moderation is the one place where almost every platform stops talking to you.

You submit something, it gets assigned a status, and that's the entire conversation. I think fixing this single thing would do more for service quality and user loyalty than most of the features these teams actually ship.

The problem isn't moderation itself. Rules exist, and somebody has to enforce them. Fine. The problem is a lack of transparency and a lack of any clear next step, at exactly the moment the user needs both.

Take submitting a post.

Pending:

The status says "pending" and stops there. Meanwhile, I'm sitting with four questions and no answers:

- What just happened? Did it go through correctly on my side?

- What am I supposed to do next? Anything at all, or nothing?

- How long does this normally take before a decision lands?

- If it sits longer than that, who do I talk to, and through which channel?

None of that needs a human. A submission timestamp, a typical decision time, an explicit "nothing is required from you", and a link to the escalation path. Four lines of copy next to a status that already exists.

Rejected:

Worse. "Your post violated our community guidelines." Which guideline? Which part of the post: the link, the wording, an image? And the question that actually matters: what do I change to get this accepted?

A rejection without instructions isn't a decision. It's a dead end. The user either gives up or resubmits blindly, gets rejected again, and now your queue is processing the same post three times because nobody told them what to fix on the first pass.

The pattern under both cases is the same: the system knows something the user doesn't and chooses not to say it. It already holds every piece of it at the moment it sets the status: the rule that fired, the part of the submission that fired it, the expected wait, the way out when that wait runs long. Withholding it usually isn't a privacy call or an anti-abuse call. It's just that nobody owns communicating the decision, only making it.

The standard defense is that detail helps bad actors tune their way past the filter. Sure, for organized spam. For everyone else, it turns a five-second fix into a support ticket, then into a user who stops posting.

Loyalty doesn't come from never getting rejected. It comes from being told why, and what to do next. Right now most platforms treat that as a support cost, when it's the cheapest retention work on the table.
