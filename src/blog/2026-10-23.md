I went through the books that keep showing up on every system design reading list these days, using the notebook flow I shared in my last post.

1. Fundamentals of Software Architecture: An Engineering Approach, Mark Richards and Neal Ford.
2. Software Architecture: The Hard Parts: Modern Trade-Off Analyses for Distributed Architectures, Neal Ford, Mark Richards, Pramod Sadalage, and Zhamak Dehghani.
3. Understanding Distributed Systems (2nd Edition), Roberto Vitillo.
4. Web Scalability for Startup Engineers: Tips & Techniques for Scaling Your Web Application, Artur Ejsmont.

What is system design? Right. It's everything.

The second one is my favorite. It describes what makes a good architect: which decisions deserve attention, and which traits it takes to be good at the job.

I agreed with most of it. A couple of things I'd add.

"Why?" isn't the only question:
Alongside "Why...?" there are "How often...?", "What if...?", and plenty more. Without that extra context, you can head in the wrong direction or miss pieces that matter. And sometimes the solution was sitting on the surface the whole time.

Redundant abstraction:
Interfaces should show up when they're actually needed, when something really has many implementations. Back when I wrote code by hand, every extra interface meant more jumping around. Not every language server can take you straight to the implementation, so you end up holding that piece in your head. And every time you land on an interface, you ask yourself: "Does this have many implementations? No? Hmm..." The fourth book even contradicts itself here: the same chapter that puts simplicity first also pushes coding to interfaces.

Beyond the technical:
The skills these books barely touch are the non-technical ones. I love listening to Kelsey Hightower talk about them: experience, letting complexity follow the business and not the other way around, learning to ask the right questions, measuring what you do. He made me rethink what I did, what I'm doing, and what I'll do next.
