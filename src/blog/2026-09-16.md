Scrum is babysitting, in my opinion and based on my experience. So why do I think this way, and why isn't it for everyone?

Continuation of this post: <https://lnkd.in/e2B5PtUc>

I read "Scrum: The Art of Doing Twice the Work in Half the Time" by Jeff Sutherland, who co-created it, then held the book next to what I actually work with every day. The gap between those two is where this opinion comes from.

In the book it's the team's own tool. The daily is there so the team can clear its own blockers. The Scrum Master is there to remove obstacles, not to collect status. The points are there to find a rhythm, not to make a promise. In practice, I see the opposite of all three: the ceremonies survive, but the intent behind them is gone.

Then there's the setup itself. On most teams I've been on, everyone was already working on their own tasks, and those tasks barely intersected. When the work is that independent, you're paying an enormous price for coordination you don't need.

The daily:

- It's useless with a team of conscientious people, professionals who own what they do. It spends their most valuable hours on a ritual.

- "What do you want to know? I'm still working. Wait for the result. If I need something, I'll ask the team, or the one person who actually owns that knowledge, directly."

- You already have the answer anyway. Do you have a tracker? Do you see the task status? Do you see the comments? Then I don't need to repeat myself. Just look.

- The price is easy to count: `15 * 5 * 4 / 60 * 9 = 45`. Fifteen minutes, five days, four weeks, nine people. That's 45 hours a month, more than a full working week, burned on status that was already written down.

Points:

- Setting points for tasks is a casino. It's sold as an abstraction away from time, and in the end you're bound to time anyway: "I'll finish this in an hour, in a day, in a week, or maybe a month, because I've never done it before and I don't know yet."

- The person doing the task is responsible for it. Not you. You're not the performer. Their experience and skills differ from yours, and their estimate comes from inside the work.

- The result should be valuable to a human: money, stability, work delivered sooner, and so on.

Once the daily and the points go, roles like "Scrum Master" and "Manager" (be specific, not "Engineering Manager") start to look thin. They should turn into something with real output, or be removed entirely.

But ceremonies aren't the disease. They're the symptom. People manipulate numbers so the board looks good. They produce work nobody needed. They copy whatever someone else used, because it's popular and "why not?" without ever reading where it came from or learning the alternatives. No critical thinking anywhere in the chain.
